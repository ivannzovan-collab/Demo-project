# Jobs to Be Done Analysis: DramaSnap

## Overview

The Jobs to Be Done (JTBD) framework, developed by Clayton Christensen and refined by Bob Moesta, Alan Klement, and others, holds that people don't buy products — they "hire" them to make progress in their lives. Understanding the job helps us design a product that customers want to hire, and identify the real competition (often not the obvious competitors).

The core JTBD insight: "People don't want a quarter-inch drill. They want a quarter-inch hole." DramaSnap users don't want a streaming app. They want to feel transported, entertained, and emotionally fulfilled in the small pockets of time their life affords them.

## Core Jobs Analysis

### Functional Jobs (The Task)

Functional jobs describe the practical task the customer is trying to accomplish. For DramaSnap users:

| Job Statement | Frequency | Urgency | Priority |
|--------------|-----------|---------|----------|
| Fill unstructured mobile time with engaging content | Daily | High | P1 |
| Follow a serialized story across multiple sessions | Daily | Medium | P1 |
| Find something to watch quickly without decision paralysis | Daily | High | P2 |
| Pass time in unavoidable waiting situations (commute, queues) | Daily | High | P1 |
| Wind down before sleep without overstimulating screen time | Daily | Medium | P2 |
| Get an emotional break from a stressful work day | Daily | Medium | P2 |
| Stay current on entertainment trends to discuss with friends | Weekly | Low | P3 |

### Emotional Jobs (The Feeling)

Emotional jobs describe how customers want to feel during and after using the product. These are often the real drivers of engagement and retention.

| Emotional Job | Description | DramaSnap Solution |
|--------------|-------------|-------------------|
| Feel transported | Escape from the mundane reality of commuting, waiting, or stress | Immersive 60-sec drama episodes with strong opening hooks |
| Feel emotionally engaged | Experience suspense, excitement, joy, or heartbreak vicariously | Cliffhanger endings; emotional arc across 100 episodes |
| Feel productive | Use "empty" time meaningfully rather than wasting it | Series progress tracking shows advancement through the story |
| Feel seen | Consume content that reflects their interests and tastes | Personalization engine that learns and adapts |
| Feel in control | Have a reliable, satisfying entertainment option always available | Offline downloads; growing catalog; predictable subscription |
| Feel connected | Share a cultural reference point with friends and community | Social sharing; fan communities; trending series |

### Social Jobs (The Perception)

Social jobs describe how customers want to be perceived by others. These drive word-of-mouth and sharing behavior.

| Social Job | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Be seen as culturally current | "Have you watched [DramaSnap series]? You need to start it." |
| Be a taste-maker | Discover a new series before friends do; recommend it to them |
| Have shared entertainment references | Participate in group conversations about drama content |
| Be seen as savvy about technology | "I use this AI drama app that personalizes the stories for you." |

## Competing Solutions Table

When a user "fires" their current solution and "hires" DramaSnap, what did they replace? Understanding competing solutions reveals the real context of comparison.

| Competing Solution | Job It Satisfies | Why Users Fire It for DramaSnap |
|-------------------|-----------------|--------------------------------|
| **ReelShort/ShortTV** | Serialized short drama on mobile | Coin economy feels manipulative; same content for everyone; catalog grows slowly |
| **TikTok/Instagram Reels** | Fill mobile time with video content | Not a story; no continuity between sessions; passive rather than intentional |
| **Netflix (short session)** | Drama with character depth | Episodes too long for micro-sessions; can't finish in a commute; TV-first design |

## Switch Forces Analysis (The Four Forces)

Bob Moesta's Four Forces of Progress model explains what drives customers to switch from an existing solution to a new one, and what holds them back.

### Push Forces (Driving Away from Current Solution)

These are the frustrations with current solutions that make users actively look for something better.

**From ReelShort/ShortTV:**
1. **Coin economy manipulation:** Paying $0.99–$1.99 per episode unlock creates anxiety about cost and resentment when the story ends at a cliffhanger. "They're making me pay to see what happens."
2. **Slow catalog growth:** Running out of content or waiting weeks for new series is frustrating for heavy users.
3. **No personalization:** After watching 20 series, the recommendations still feel generic and not tailored to your taste.
4. **Quality inconsistency:** Some series are dramatically better than others, and there's no reliable signal for what's worth watching.

**From TikTok/Instagram:**
1. **Hollow satisfaction:** Users report feeling like they wasted time after a passive scroll session. "I watched TikTok for 30 minutes and I don't even remember what I saw."
2. **No story continuity:** Every session starts from zero; there's no "picking up where I left off" satisfaction.
3. **Algorithm manipulation feeling:** TikTok feels designed to keep you watching in ways that don't feel good. "It's like a slot machine."
4. **No progress:** Watching TikTok doesn't feel like progress toward anything. Finishing an episode arc does.

**From Netflix (for micro-sessions):**
1. **Episode length mismatch:** A Netflix episode is 45–60 minutes. You have 12 minutes on the train. You can't even start something you can't finish.
2. **Decision paralysis:** Netflix's enormous catalog creates choice anxiety. "I spent 20 minutes deciding what to watch and then had to stop."
3. **"TV mode" design:** Netflix is designed for the TV in the living room. Using it on a phone in a commute feels like a degraded experience.

### Pull Forces (Attracting Toward DramaSnap)

These are the specific features and experiences that attract users to DramaSnap.

1. **Designed for mobile:** Content that looks and feels native on a phone screen.
2. **60-second episodes:** You can always watch "one more." You always know how long an episode takes. You can finish an episode even if you only have 2 minutes.
3. **Subscription simplicity:** Flat monthly fee; no per-episode charges; predictable cost.
4. **Gets smarter:** The promise that DramaSnap learns your taste creates a "pull" that increases over time — the longer you use it, the better it gets.
5. **Always new content:** AI production means the catalog grows daily, eliminating the "I've watched everything" problem.

### Anxiety Forces (Holding Back from Switching)

These are the concerns and doubts that prevent users from trying DramaSnap, even when they're frustrated with current solutions.

1. **"AI content must be low quality":** The perception that AI-generated drama is cheap, uncanny, or unsatisfying. This is the #1 anxiety to address.
2. **"I'll lose my progress on ReelShort":** Users invested in ongoing series on competitor platforms don't want to abandon them. DramaSnap needs compelling enough new series to justify the context switch.
3. **"Another subscription I'll forget to cancel":** Subscription fatigue is real. Users worry about adding another recurring charge.
4. **"What if I use it once and never go back?":** Fear of wasted money if the product doesn't stick.

**Mitigation strategies:**
- Free tier (no payment required to start) removes subscription anxiety
- 7-day free trial for premium reduces commitment concern
- High-quality "signature series" as the first impression addresses quality anxiety
- Series recommendation that accounts for ReelShort genre preferences addresses "starting over" anxiety

### Habit Forces (Inertia of Current Solution)

These are the established habits and behaviors that keep users with their current solution, even when they're dissatisfied.

1. **TikTok as the default:** Opening TikTok when bored is an automatic behavior. DramaSnap needs to insert itself into that trigger-behavior-reward loop.
2. **ReelShort coin collection:** Users who have accumulated coins or have ongoing series feel "sunk cost" attachment to the platform.
3. **Netflix as "real" entertainment:** Netflix has cultural cachet as "where quality content is." Changing this perception takes time.
4. **Phone placement habits:** Which apps are on your home screen drives what you open automatically.

**Mitigation strategies:**
- Push notifications designed to interrupt the TikTok habit at the trigger moment ("Your commute is about to start. Continue your series.")
- Make DramaSnap the first icon in the entertainment folder (through onboarding UX suggestions)
- Social features that create FOMO ("3 of your contacts are watching [series name]")

## Customer Interview Guide

These 10 questions are designed to surface JTBD insights through interviews with target customers. They focus on specific past behavior rather than hypothetical preferences.

### Interview Instructions
- Conduct interviews 1:1, 30–45 minutes each
- Focus on specific recent events, not generalized opinions
- Ask "tell me about the last time..." not "do you usually..."
- Probe the emotional context of moments, not just the functional task

---

**Question 1:** Think about the last time you opened a drama or entertainment app on your phone. What were you doing before you opened it? What was going on around you?

*Purpose: Identify the trigger context — what prompts the switch to entertainment consumption*

---

**Question 2:** Take me back to the moment you first downloaded [ReelShort/ShortTV/the app they currently use for short drama]. What happened right before you downloaded it? What were you hoping it would do for you?

*Purpose: Identify the "first thought" moment and the initial job being hired for*

---

**Question 3:** Walk me through the last series you watched from beginning to end. Tell me about the experience — when did you watch it, how many episodes per session, how long did it take to finish?

*Purpose: Understand actual consumption patterns, session structure, pacing preferences*

---

**Question 4:** Tell me about a time when you stopped watching a series midway through and didn't finish it. What happened? What made you give up?

*Purpose: Identify the failure modes — what causes users to "fire" a product or a specific piece of content*

---

**Question 5:** When you're about to watch something on your commute or during a break, what goes through your head as you decide what to watch? How long does it usually take to decide? How do you feel when you can't decide quickly?

*Purpose: Surface the "choice paralysis" problem; identify decision-making triggers*

---

**Question 6:** Have you ever recommended a drama series to a friend or family member? What was that like? What made you want to share it?

*Purpose: Identify the social job and what triggers word-of-mouth recommendation*

---

**Question 7:** Tell me about a moment when you felt really satisfied after watching something on your phone. What was the content? What made it satisfying? How did you feel afterward?

*Purpose: Understand the emotional "hired" outcome — what success feels like*

---

**Question 8:** Have you tried any AI-generated content — writing, images, video? What did you think? How did you feel about it?

*Purpose: Surface attitudes toward AI-generated content to understand the anxiety force*

---

**Question 9:** If I told you there's a streaming app that costs $6.99/month, produces AI-generated drama in 60-second episodes, and learns what you like over time — what's your first reaction? What would make you want to try it? What would make you hesitate?

*Purpose: Direct test of the DramaSnap concept against switch forces*

---

**Question 10:** What's your biggest frustration with how you currently spend entertainment time on your phone? If you could change one thing about how you consume drama or story content on mobile, what would it be?

*Purpose: Open-ended frustration capture; often surfaces insights that structured questions miss*

## Job Stories

Job stories follow the format: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]." They are more useful than user stories because they center context and outcome rather than task and role.

### Job Story 1: The Commuter

**Situation:** I'm on the subway platform waiting for my train, then on the train for 18 minutes. I have my earbuds in and my phone in hand.

**Job Story:** When I board the train for my morning commute, I want to immediately drop into a story I've been following so I can feel like the travel time was well spent and arrive at work having had a moment of enjoyment that was just for me.

**Design Implications:**
- "Continue watching" should be the first thing on the home screen
- The episode should start playing within 2 taps of opening the app
- 18 minutes fits 15–18 episodes — the session should feel naturally complete at common commute lengths
- Offline caching should auto-download the next 10 episodes while on WiFi

---

### Job Story 2: The Insomniac

**Situation:** It's 11:30 PM. I can't fall asleep. I'm in bed with my phone, looking for something to watch that will relax me but not overstimulate me.

**Job Story:** When I can't sleep and I pick up my phone, I want something engaging enough to stop me from thinking about tomorrow's problems but not so exciting that it keeps me awake, so I can drift off feeling entertained rather than anxious.

**Design Implications:**
- A "Sleep Mode" that dims the screen, reduces episode intensity flagging, and auto-stops after X episodes
- Recommend slower-paced romance or drama for late-night sessions (inferred from time of day + behavioral patterns)
- Do not send push notifications during typical sleep hours

---

### Job Story 3: The Work Break

**Situation:** It's 2:30 PM on a stressful work day. I have 10 minutes before my next meeting. I need to mentally reset.

**Job Story:** When I'm mid-day stressed and have a short break, I want to be quickly transported out of work mode and into a compelling story so I can return to work refreshed and with a mental boundary between my last meeting and my next one.

**Design Implications:**
- Cold open first episode of recommended series — no skip intro, no loading screen, immediate immersion
- 10 minutes = 8–10 episodes — design satisfying "mini-arc" resolutions within 8 episodes
- The experience should feel like a complete mental vacation, not a teaser that leaves you frustrated

---

### Job Story 4: The Drama Craving

**Situation:** I've been looking forward to my lunch break all morning. I want to watch drama. Not just "something" — specifically melodrama with romantic tension.

**Job Story:** When I have deliberately set aside time to enjoy drama on my lunch break, I want to find exactly the right series that matches my current mood and craving (slow-burn romance, villain redemption arc, etc.) in under 30 seconds of browsing, so I can spend my limited time watching rather than choosing.

**Design Implications:**
- Mood-based browsing UI ("I'm in the mood for..." selector)
- Personalization should account for time-of-day mood patterns
- Series recommendation should surface specifically the genre the user has been watching most

---

### Job Story 5: The Trendsetter

**Situation:** My friend group talks about entertainment constantly. I want to be the one who surfaces the next "thing everyone's watching."

**Job Story:** When I'm looking for new content, I want to discover something that feels fresh and not yet mainstream, so I can recommend it to my friends before it blows up and be known as the one who finds the good stuff.

**Design Implications:**
- "Rising" or "Just discovered by early watchers" content section
- Shareable "I just started watching this" moment with preview clip
- Creator profiles that surface new series from creators with a track record
- Social sharing designed around "be the first to share this"

---

### Job Story 6: The Watercooler

**Situation:** My coworkers talk about shows all the time and I feel left out because I watch things at different times on different platforms.

**Job Story:** When I'm trying to connect with coworkers around entertainment, I want a shared cultural reference that the people around me are also watching, so I can participate in the conversation and feel socially connected through shared entertainment experience.

**Design Implications:**
- "Trending in your city" or "Your contacts are watching" social signals
- Community watch features that create synchronized viewing around new episode drops
- Notification: "Episode 50 of [series] just dropped — the cliffhanger everyone's talking about"

---

### Job Story 7: The Choice Paralyzer

**Situation:** I have 20 minutes to watch something. I spend 15 minutes scrolling through Netflix unable to decide and give up without watching anything.

**Job Story:** When I sit down to watch something and I'm not sure what I want, I want to be given one strong recommendation that I trust and can start immediately, so I can spend my time watching instead of choosing.

**Design Implications:**
- "Play something for me" button — single-tap to start the algorithmically best recommendation
- Remove low-confidence recommendations from the home screen; show only high-match content
- "Your pick today" — one highlighted recommendation per day based on mood, time, and recent viewing

---

### Job Story 8: The Guilt-Free Viewer

**Situation:** I want to watch drama but I feel guilty about "wasting time" on something that feels frivolous or embarrassing.

**Job Story:** When I choose to watch drama on my phone, I want to feel like I'm doing something enjoyable and valuable with my time rather than something shameful, so I can fully relax into the entertainment experience without a running commentary of self-judgment.

**Design Implications:**
- Never gamify in ways that feel manipulative or exploitative (no "you've been watching for 3 hours" warnings framed as shame)
- Position entertainment as self-care, not waste
- Celebrate series completions as an achievement — "You finished the series!" rather than tracking total watch time
- Avoid dark patterns that manufacture addiction

---

### Job Story 9: The Quality Seeker

**Situation:** I've tried AI-generated content before and found it generic, strange-looking, or emotionally flat. I'm skeptical of DramaSnap but open to being surprised.

**Job Story:** When I try a new entertainment platform that uses AI-generated content, I want to immediately see that the quality exceeds my expectations, so I can feel that my time was well spent and share the discovery with my friends.

**Design Implications:**
- First experience must be curated around the highest-quality "signature series"
- Onboarding should not show the breadth of the catalog before showing the depth of the best content
- Quality signals should be surfaced early (completion rate, ratings, "most loved by people like you")
- Consider a "convinced me" moment — a specific episode designed to be the quality proof point
